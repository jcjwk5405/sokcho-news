Editor's Letter

Saying Sorry Is Easy; Fixing Things Takes Proof

This week's newspaper editorials all covered President Lee Jae-myung's press conference on September 18, the confirmation hearings for cabinet nominees, and the first U.S. interest rate hike in over three years. Gallup Korea's latest poll shows 56% of people disapprove of the president's job performance and 37% approve. The top reason for disapproval is real estate policy (20%), followed by personnel choices (16%). Note that the poll was taken before the press conference.

The saying we chose this week means "if you know your mistake, you must fix it." The president admitted he had fallen short, and the papers asked whether that will lead to real changes. This week's editorial says something similar about local promises: a promise is proven by whether it appears in the budget.

In the region, Sokcho reported 16.9 billion won in national funding for water supply projects, and Goseong said its projects will receive 27.8 billion won in new national funding in next year's government budget. Yangyang approved a 477.3 billion won supplementary budget. Please note: there will be no issue on September 28 because of the Chuseok holiday. The next issue comes out on October 5.

This Week's Terms

Words in the News, Explained

Three terms that came up often in this week's national newspaper editorials — explained simply, so you can follow the news even if you're new to Korean politics and policy.

Confirmation Hearing ReportPOLITICS

A report the National Assembly committee writes after a hearing, giving its opinion on whether a nominee is fit. A cabinet minister can be appointed even without the Assembly's approval, so this report is not required for the appointment.

Monetary TighteningECONOMY

A policy where a central bank raises interest rates or reduces the money in circulation to control prices. When rates rise, loans cost more. This word appeared often this week after the U.S. Federal Reserve raised rates.

White-Collar ExemptionLABOR

A rule from the United States that exempts higher-paid managers and professionals from weekly working-hour limits and overtime pay rules. Whether to adopt it is a key question in the talks about special labor rules for Korea's "mega special zones."
